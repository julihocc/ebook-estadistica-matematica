      \begin{block}{The Brute-Force Dilemma (Census)}
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.1cm
          \item In engineering, economics, and life sciences, determining exact parameters across massive populations by measuring every unit is infeasible:
          \begin{itemize}
            \item Prohibitive logistical and computational costs.
            \item Destructive quality testing (e.g., tensile strength of steel beams).
            \item Conceptually infinite target populations.
          \end{itemize}
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{The Statistical Solution: SRS Inference}
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.1cm
          \item We extract a representative subset of size $n \ll N$ under rigorous conditions of randomness.
          \item We compute a \textbf{sample statistic} ($\bar{X}, S^2$) to approximate and bound the \textbf{population parameter} ($\mu, \sigma^2$) with known probability.
          \item We transform qualitative uncertainty into quantifiable, bounded statistical risk.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

% Slide 04: Simple Random Sampling (SRS) and Statistical Independence
\begin{frame}{Simple Random Sampling (SRS) and Statistical Independence}
  \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{Formal Definition of Simple Random Sampling (SRS)}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    A collection of random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ constitutes a \textbf{simple random sample of size $n$} drawn from the population $f_X(x)$ if they simultaneously fulfill two axiomatic conditions:
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep=0.1cm
      \item \textbf{Identical Distribution:} Each individual variable $X_i$ shares the exact same marginal probability distribution, density, and theoretical moments as $X$: $f_{X_i}(x) = f_X(x)$, with $E(X_i) = \mu$ and $\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2$.
      \item \textbf{Mutual Independence:} The observation of any subset of variables does not alter the conditional distribution of the remaining variables. The joint probability density factors perfectly:
      $$f_{X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n}(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n f_X(x_i)$$
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{Statistics vs. Parameters}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    A \textbf{parameter} is a fixed but unknown real constant of the population ($\mu, \sigma^2$). A \textbf{statistic} is a measurable function of the random sample $T(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ involving no unknown constants ($\bar{X}, S^2$). Being a function of random variables, every statistic is itself a random variable with its own probability distribution, known as its \emph{sampling distribution}.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 05: Sampling With vs Without Replacement (FPC Factor)
\begin{frame}{Sampling With vs Without Replacement (FPC Factor)}
  \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{Expected Value and Variance in Infinite Populations (or With Replacement)}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    If sampling is performed with replacement or from an infinite population ($N \to \infty$), the variables $X_i$ are strictly i.i.d. By linearity of expectation and additivity of variance for independent variables:
    $$E(\bar{X}) = E\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \frac{1}{n}(n\mu) = \mu, \qquad \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \text{Var}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \frac{1}{n^2}(n\sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{The Finite Population Correction (FPC) Factor}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size $N$, the variables $X_i$ exhibit a slight negative correlation induced by census depletion ($\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = -\frac{\sigma^2}{N-1}$). This dependency strictly reduces the sample mean variance via the \textbf{Finite Population Correction (FPC) factor}:
    $$\text{Var}_{W/O}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \cdot \left(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\right) \implies \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}$$
    If the sampling fraction $n/N \le 0.05$ ($5\%$), the factor $(N-n)/(N-1) \approx 1$ and the FPC is safely omitted in practice.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 06: The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)
\begin{frame}{The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)}
  \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN — Khinchin)}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be an i.i.d. random sample drawn from a population with finite theoretical mean $\mu < \infty$. For any arbitrarily small margin of tolerance $\epsilon > 0$, the probability that the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ deviates from the population parameter $\mu$ by $\epsilon$ or more tends rigorously to zero as the sample size $n$ increases:
    $$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) = 0 \quad \iff \quad \bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu \quad \text{(Convergence in Probability)}$$
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN — Kolmogorov)}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    Under the exact same assumption of a finite first-order moment, the sequence of sample means converges to the true population parameter $\mu$ with probability exactly equal to one:
    $$P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1 \quad \iff \quad \bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu \quad \text{(Almost Sure Convergence)}$$
    \textbf{Operational Meaning:} Empirical uncertainty regarding the center of gravity of a random phenomenon vanishes asymptotically when accumulating sufficient independent observations.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 07: The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
\begin{frame}{The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)}
  \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{Classic Lindeberg-Lévy Statement}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be a simple random sample (i.i.d.) drawn from a population with theoretical mean $\mu$ and strictly positive, finite variance $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$. As the sample size $n$ grows indefinitely, the standardized random variable $Z_n$ associated with the sample mean converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$, \textbf{regardless of the shape or skewness of the underlying population distribution}:
    $$\lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(Z_n \le z\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P\left(\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \le z\right) = \Phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{z} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt$$
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{The Universal Scope of the CLT}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.08cm
      \item \textbf{Asymptotic Approximation of the Mean:} For sufficiently large $n$ ($n \ge 30$ as a standard empirical rule of thumb), the sample mean can be treated as normal: $\bar{X}_n \dot{\sim} N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$.
      \item \textbf{Approximation of the Sample Sum:} Analogously, the total sum $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ converges to a normal distribution: $S_n \dot{\sim} N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 08: Asymptotic Anatomy and Berry-Esseen Bound
\begin{frame}{Asymptotic Anatomy and Rate of Convergence}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The Empirical $n \ge 30$ Rule}
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.1cm
          \item If the original population is normal, $\bar{X}_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ exactly for any $n \ge 1$.
          \item If the population is uniform, symmetric, or continuous with finite variance, $n \ge 15$ usually suffices for excellent normality.
          \item If the population is highly skewed (exponential, Pareto), $n \ge 30$ to $n \ge 100$ is required to stabilize tail probabilities.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Universal Berry-Esseen Bound}
        \small
        \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.1cm
          \item To mathematically bound the maximum discrepancy between the empirical CDF $F_n(z)$ and $\Phi(z)$, the absolute third central moment $\rho_3 = E[|X - \mu|^3] < \infty$ is required:
          $$\sup_{z \in \mathbb{R}} |F_n(z) - \Phi(z)| \le \frac{C \cdot \rho_3}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}}$$
          \item The approximation error decays at rate $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, where $C < 0.4784$.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 1: Fundamental (1/2)}
  \begin{block}{Problem 2.6.3 --- Reduction of the Sample Mean Standard Error}
    \footnotesize
    The assembly time of a precision mechanical component on an automotive manufacturing line follows a theoretical probability distribution with population mean $\mu = 45$ minutes and standard deviation $\sigma = 12$ minutes. If a process engineer draws a simple random sample of size $n$ to estimate shift productivity:
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep=0.15cm
      \item Compute the exact standard error of the sample mean, denoted algebraically by $\text{SE}(\bar{X}) = \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, across the following sample sizes:
      $$n \in \{9, 36, 144, 576\}$$
      \item Explain formally why quadrupling the sample size does not divide the estimator's uncertainty by four, identifying the governing mathematical rate of decay.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

% Slide 10B: In-Class Exercise — Tier 1: Fundamental (Solution)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 1: Fundamental (2/2)}
  \fontsize{6.8pt}{8.2pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{1. Exact Calculation of the Standard Error ($\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$)}
    \fontsize{6.8pt}{8.2pt}\selectfont
    Substituting $\sigma = 12$ directly and extracting the exact square root for each sample size:
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.06cm
      \item For $n = 9$: $\displaystyle \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{12}{3} = \mathbf{4.00 \text{ min}}$.
      \item For $n = 36$: $\displaystyle \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{12}{6} = \mathbf{2.00 \text{ min}}$.
      \item For $n = 144$: $\displaystyle \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{144}} = \frac{12}{12} = \mathbf{1.00 \text{ min}}$.
      \item For $n = 576$: $\displaystyle \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{12}{\sqrt{576}} = \frac{12}{24} = \mathbf{0.50 \text{ min}}$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{2. Law of Diminishing Returns ($1/\sqrt{n}$)}
    \fontsize{6.8pt}{8.2pt}\selectfont
    Observing the sequence $(4 \to 2 \to 1 \to 0.5)$, we notice that each time the sample size $n$ is multiplied by a factor of $4$ ($9 \times 4 = 36$, $36 \times 4 = 144$, etc.), the standard error is halved ($\sigma_{\bar{X}}/2$). This stems from the square root denominator ($\sqrt{4n} = 2\sqrt{n}$). In engineering, doubling statistical precision requires a fourfold increase in data acquisition investment.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 11A: In-Class Exercise — Tier 2: Operational (Statement)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 2: Operational (1/2)}
  \begin{block}{Problem 2.6.4 --- Government Audit and the FPC Factor}
    \footnotesize
    A government audit evaluates the monthly revenues of a closed commercial guild containing exactly $N = 400$ registered enterprises. The population standard deviation is historically established from past censuses at $\sigma = \$15,000$ MXN. An audit team randomly selects a sample of $n = 64$ firms.
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep=0.15cm
      \item Compute the variance of the sample mean $\text{Var}(\bar{X})$ under the assumption of sampling \textbf{with replacement} ($\text{Var}_{W/R}$).
      \item Compute the variance of the sample mean $\text{Var}(\bar{X})$ under realistic sampling \textbf{without replacement}, applying the finite population correction factor $\text{FPC} = \frac{N-n}{N-1}$ ($\text{Var}_{W/O}$).
      \item What exact percentage reduction in the sample mean revenue variance is achieved by virtue of the finite population correction factor in this auditing scenario?
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

% Slide 11B: In-Class Exercise — Tier 2: Operational (Solution)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 2: Operational (2/2)}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{1. Variance With Replacement ($\text{Var}_{W/R}$)}
        \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
        Applying the standard formula for independent variables:
        $$\text{Var}_{W/R}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = \frac{(15,000)^2}{64}$$
        $$\text{Var}_{W/R}(\bar{X}) = \frac{225,000,000}{64} = \mathbf{3,515,625 \text{ MXN}^2}$$
        The associated standard error is $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \sqrt{3,515,625} = \$1,875$ MXN.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{2. Variance Without Replacement ($\text{Var}_{W/O}$)}
        \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
        Evaluating the FPC factor for $N=400, n=64$:
        $$\text{FPC} = \frac{N-n}{N-1} = \frac{400-64}{400-1} = \frac{336}{399} \approx 0.842105$$
        Multiplying by the baseline variance:
        $$\text{Var}_{W/O}(\bar{X}) = 3,515,625 \times \frac{336}{399} = \mathbf{2,960,150.38 \text{ MXN}^2}$$
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{-0.1cm}
  \pause
  \begin{exampleblock}{3. Percentage Reduction in Estimator Variance}
    \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
    The variance fraction eliminated by the finite population correction is precisely $1 - \text{FPC}$:
    $$\Delta\% = \left(1 - \frac{336}{399}\right) \times 100\% = \frac{63}{399} \times 100\% = \frac{3}{19} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{15.79\%}$$
    By sampling $16\%$ of the guild's population ($64/400$), the negative covariance among distinct selections eliminates $15.79\%$ of estimation variance relative to an infinite sampling assumption.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 12A: In-Class Exercise — Tier 3: Analytical (Statement)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 3: Analytical (1/2)}
  \begin{block}{Problem 2.6.8 --- Algebraic Proof of Unbiasedness for Sample Variance $S^2$}
    \footnotesize
    Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be a simple random sample (SRS) of independent and identically distributed variables with finite theoretical mean $E(X_i) = \mu$ and finite theoretical variance $\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2$. We define the sample variance by dividing by $n-1$ degrees of freedom:
    $$S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$$
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep=0.15cm
      \item Prove algebraically step-by-step, applying the linear properties of the expectation operator, that $S^2$ is an \textbf{unbiased estimator} of the population variance, meaning that $E(S^2) = \sigma^2$.
      \item \emph{Algebraic Hint:} Add and subtract the true population mean $\mu$ inside the squared deviation: $(X_i - \bar{X})^2 = [(X_i - \mu) - (\bar{X} - \mu)]^2$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

% Slide 12B: In-Class Exercise — Tier 3: Analytical (Solution)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 3: Analytical (2/2)}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{1. Algebraic Expansion}
        \fontsize{6.2pt}{7.4pt}\selectfont
        We expand the binomial term inside the total sum by adding and subtracting $\mu$:
        $$\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n [(X_i - \mu) - (\bar{X} - \mu)]^2$$
        Distributing the sum, since $(\bar{X}-\mu)$ is a common factor in the middle term:
        $$= \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 - 2(\bar{X} - \mu)\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu) + n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$$
        Since $\sum (X_i - \mu) = n(\bar{X} - \mu)$, we substitute:
        $$\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$$
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{2. Expectation and Degrees of Freedom}
        \fontsize{6.2pt}{7.4pt}\selectfont
        Taking expectation while noting $E[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $E[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2] = \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$:
        $$E\left[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \right] = n\sigma^2 - n\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) = (n - 1)\sigma^2$$
        Substituting back into the definition of $S^2$:
        $$E(S^2) = \frac{1}{n-1} E\left[ \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 \right] = \frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n-1} = \mathbf{\sigma^2 \quad \blacksquare}$$
        Dividing by $n$ would systematically underestimate variance by $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% Slide 13A: In-Class Exercise — Tier 4: Challenging (Statement)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 4: Challenging (1/2)}
  \begin{block}{Problem 2.6.9 --- Breakdown of the CLT in Heavy-Tailed Systems (Cauchy)}
    \footnotesize
    The Central Limit Theorem postulates that the standardized sample mean of independent random variables converges to a normal distribution whenever the second central moment ($\sigma^2$) is finite. Consider a population governed by the \textbf{Standard Cauchy Distribution}, whose probability density function is:
    $$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}, \quad x \in (-\infty, \infty)$$
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep=0.15cm
      \item Prove analytically why the integral defining the population expectation $E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) \, dx$ fails to converge absolutely, implying infinite theoretical variance.
      \item Given that the characteristic function of the standard Cauchy distribution is $\varphi_X(t) = e^{-|t|}$, determine the exact distribution of the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ and conclude whether convergence toward normality occurs as $n \to \infty$.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

% Slide 13B: In-Class Exercise — Tier 4: Challenging (Solution)
\begin{frame}{In-Class Exercise --- Tier 4: Challenging (2/2)}
  \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{1. Divergence of Expected Value Integral and Heavy Tails}
    \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
    We evaluate absolute convergence over the non-negative real half-line $[0, \infty)$:
    $$\int_{0}^{\infty} |x| f(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = \lim_{M \to \infty} \left[ \frac{1}{2\pi} \ln(1+x^2) \right]_{0}^{M} = \lim_{M \to \infty} \frac{\ln(1+M^2)}{2\pi} = +\infty$$
    Because the absolute integral diverges to $+\infty$, the improper integral $E(X)$ is mathematically undefined. Decaying only as $O(1/x^2)$, Cauchy tails generate extreme outliers so frequently that theoretical variance diverges ($\sigma^2 = \infty$).
  \end{block}
  \vspace{-0.15cm}
  \pause
  \begin{alertblock}{2. Invariance of the Sample Mean ($\bar{X}_n \sim \text{Cauchy}(0,1)$)}
    \fontsize{6.5pt}{7.8pt}\selectfont
    By independence, the characteristic function of the sum $S_n = \sum X_i$ factors: $\varphi_{S_n}(t) = [\varphi_X(t)]^n = [e^{-|t|}]^n = e^{-n|t|}$.
    For the sample average $\bar{X}_n = S_n / n$, we apply the scaling property in the frequency domain argument:
    $$\varphi_{\bar{X}_n}(t) = \varphi_{S_n}\left(\frac{t}{n}\right) = e^{-n\left|\frac{t}{n}\right|} = e^{-|t|} = \varphi_X(t)$$
    \textbf{Mathematical Conclusion:} The sample mean possesses exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single individual observation ($\mathbf{\bar{X}_n \sim \text{Cauchy}(0,1)}$). Averaging $n=1,000,000$ independent Cauchy measurements \textbf{yields zero reduction in dispersion and never converges to a normal curve}. The CLT completely shatters when $\sigma^2 = \infty$!
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% Slide 14: Synthesis and Conclusion
\begin{frame}{Synthesis and Conclusion --- Random Sampling and CLT}
  \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
  \begin{block}{Conceptual Pillars of Section 02.06}
    \fontsize{7.5pt}{9pt}\selectfont
    \begin{itemize}\itemsep=0.12cm
      \item \textbf{Simple Random Sampling (SRS):} An i.i.d. random sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ enables the construction of unbiased sample statistics to bound uncertainty concerning population parameters.
      \item \textbf{FPC Factor in Finite Populations:} Sampling without replacement across a census fraction strictly reduces estimator variance: $\text{FPC} = \frac{N-n}{N-1}$.
      \item \textbf{Asymptotic Stability (LLN):} The Law of Large Numbers guarantees that the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ converges in probability (WLLN) and almost surely (SLLN) toward $\mu$.
      \item \textbf{Universal Normality (CLT):} Provided $\sigma^2 < \infty$, standardized sample means $Z_n$ converge to $N(0,1)$ for large sample sizes ($n \ge 30$), irrespective of initial population skewness.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}
